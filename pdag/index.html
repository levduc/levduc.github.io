A Transaction-Level Model for Blockchain Privacy

A privacy coin tells you your payment is hidden among 16. Among how many is it really hidden? We build one graph model, the PDAG, that makes this question precise. With it, we put Coinjoin, Tornado Cash, Monero and Zcash side by side.

François-Xavier Wicht1, Zhipeng Wang2, Duc V. Le3, Christian Cachin1

1University of Bern  ·  2Imperial College London  ·  3Visa Research (main part of the work done at the University of Bern)

1 · The puzzle

Three private transactions. Can you trace them?

Each transaction below hides the coin it really spent among decoys. From the outside you only see which coins it might have spent: $w_1$ and $w_2$ each point at two, and $w_3$ at three. That makes their anonymity sets 2, 2 and 3. Pick the coin you think each one spent, then reveal.

    Whatever $w_1$ and $w_2$ spent, between them they used up $s_1$ and $s_2$. Every transaction spends at least one coin, and no coin is spent twice. That leaves $w_3$ exactly one option, $s_3$. The anonymity set said 3. The true count is 1. The rest of this post builds a definition of untraceability that catches this.

    This is Figure 3 of the paper. Its ground truth is the matching $M_1$. The other matching, $M_2$, is equally consistent with what the adversary sees, so no one can do better than a coin flip on $w_1$ and $w_2$.

    What this post shows

    Claim 1

    Anonymity sets overstate untraceability. Count only the candidates that survive pruning to the graph's core. A set of 3 can hide nothing (Defs. 5–7).

    See it →
    Claim 2

    Hiding the coin and hiding the owner are different properties. Coinjoin and Tornado Cash give unlinkability, but no untraceability at all (§4.1).

    See it →
    Claim 3

    One notation fits four systems. Their guarantees line up in one table. For example, Monero's rings of 16 give 16-untraceability when its graph equals its core (Lemma 6).

    2 · Why did that happen? · Figure 2

    A picture in which spending can hide

    To reason about the puzzle, we need a picture of transactions in which a spend can be hidden. We start from the Transaction DAG of Cachin et al. Coins are states ($\bigcirc$), each transaction has one witness ($\Box$), and edges say whether a state is consumed, only read (observed), or produced. We add two things. A masking edge consumes a state, but to everyone except its author it looks like a mere read. Nullifiers ($\Diamond$) stop the same coin from being spent twice; in practice they are Monero key images, Zcash nullifiers and Tornado Cash withdrawal commitments. Switch to the adversary's view, and every masking edge blends into the ambiguous ones.

      3 · The definition that falls out · Definitions 5–7

      Prune first, then count

      The puzzle generalises. Keep only the ordinary transactions and their candidate coins, and you get a bipartite graph $G^*$. The real spends form a maximum matching, so any edge that lies in no maximum matching can be thrown away. What remains is the Dulmage–Mendelsohn core (Def. 5). A transaction is $k$-untraceable when $k\ge 2$ of its edges survive (Def. 6), and a whole ledger is only as untraceable as its weakest transaction (Def. 7). Edit the graph and watch which edges survive the pruning.

        4 · Hiding the coin is not hiding the owner · Figure 6 · Lemma 1

        Coinjoin: every spend in plain sight, owners blurred

        Untraceability asks which coin a transaction spent. Unlinkability asks which coins belong to the same person, which is a different question. Bitcoin analysts link coins with the multi-input heuristic (Eq. 1): every input of a transaction has one owner. Coinjoin breaks that on purpose. In Figure 6, three users merge 7 inputs. Every spend is visible, so there is no untraceability at all, yet the adversary's "one owner" guess is wrong. The unlinkability score (Def. 10) measures how wrong.           The paper's split reproduces its stated scores: $\frac{10}{21}$ for the four inputs of the two 2-address users, and $\frac{6}{21}$ for the three inputs of the 3-address user (§4.1.1). Lemma 1 bounds a Coinjoin with at least two owners between $\binom{|S_I|-|U|}{2}/\binom{|S_I|}{2}$ and $\binom{|S_I|-|u|}{2}/\binom{|S_I|}{2}$, where $U$ is the largest owner class and $u$ the smallest. The bars use the formula of Def. 10, and the dashed line applies its wording to the whole class. The two differ; Appendix A compares them.

          5 · Four systems, one alphabet · Figures 8 and 9

          Monero and Zcash, drawn the same way

          Now we can put real systems into the same picture. Monero and Zcash both hide the spent coin behind masking edges and stop double-spends with nullifiers. They differ in who ends up in the untraceability set. A Monero ring signature names its decoys explicitly, with 16 ring members since v0.18.0.0. A Zcash shielded spend names none, so the set is implicitly every earlier state in the pool. Step through each chain's sample run from the paper.

            6 · Where this goes next · §5

            From "who could have spent it" to "how likely"

            Everything so far is deterministic: an edge is either a candidate or it is not. Real adversaries also have side information, such as timing, amounts, or a machine-learning model. Section 5 of the paper sketches the probabilistic version. Each edge $e$ of $G^*$ gets a weight $\omega(e)\in[0,1]$, the probability that the state was consumed by that witness, and the weights of each witness sum to 1. The adversary then guesses by weight. Below, the core of the puzzle graph has exactly two maximum matchings left, so a single number sets every weight: the adversary's belief that $M_1$, not $M_2$, is the true one.

            • edge width and label = weight $\omega(e)$

            This is an illustration of the extension. The paper defines $\omega$ and stops there; it does not analyse weighted untraceability. The link between the weights ($p$ for $M_1$'s edges, $1-p$ for $M_2$'s) follows only from this core having exactly those two maximum matchings.

            Appendix A · Definition 10 · Lemmas 2–4, 7

            The unlinkability score, and the paper's two readings of it

            The adversary puts $n$ states into one class $[s]_{\tilde L}$. In this calculator, $l$ of them form a single group that truly belongs together, and the other $n-l$ are unrelated singletons. Definition 10 describes the score in words as "the ratio of the number of unlinked pairs to all possible pairs". Its formula, however, counts only the pairs inside $[s]_{\tilde L}\setminus[s]_L$. The two agree in some places and not in others. The paper's worked values also split between them, so this calculator shows both: (A) is the formula for a member of the group, and (B) is the wording applied to the whole class.

            Jump to a case from the paper
            • (A) Def. 10 formula, for a group member
            • (B) Def. 10 wording, whole class: unlinked pairs / all pairs
            Table view — every place the paper gives a score

            (A) $=\binom{|[s]_{\tilde L}\setminus[s]_L|}{2}\big/\binom{|[s]_{\tilde L}|}{2}$, as written in Def. 10. (B) $=\big(\binom{n}{2}-p\big)/\binom{n}{2}$ over the whole class, where $p$ is the number of truly linked pairs: the Def. 10 wording, and the form used in the proofs of Lemmas 2, 4 and 7. Where the two kinds of state differ, (A) is given for both. $[s]_L$ always contains $s$, because $L$ is an equivalence relation (Def. 8). Fractions are left unreduced, as in the paper.
