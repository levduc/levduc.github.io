§1Prelude

The public-key cryptography most of us learned first rests on factoring or discrete logs (ElGamal to encrypt, Schnorr to sign), and a quantum computer would be able to break all of it §1, p. 2. So what do we encrypt and sign with instead?

Two of the schemes NIST standardized are built on lattices: Kyber (ML-KEM) for encryption and Dilithium (ML-DSA) for signatures. They are less foreign than they look. The encryption scheme follows the outline of ElGamal and the signature scheme is an analogue of Schnorr §1, pp. 3–4; §2.2–2.3, p. 6. What changes is the hard problem underneath: where discrete log hides $s$ inside $g^s$, lattices hide a short vector $\mathbf{s}$ inside a matrix product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$ with a little noise added.

So this page asks one question: can we rebuild ElGamal and Schnorr ourselves from noisy linear algebra, and why would anyone believe the result is hard to break? §2 rebuilds encryption with plain matrices, §3 asks why it should be hard, §4 swaps numbers for polynomials to make it small and fast (Kyber), and §5 signs (Dilithium).

Kyber (ML-KEM) and Dilithium (ML-DSA) need few deep concepts; the mess is in parameters and rounding tricks. §1, pp. 2–3

The paper’s exact statement

The road to the NIST standards

Modern lattice cryptography starts with two works from the mid 1990s. Ajtai [Ajt96] showed that solving a random instance of the Small Integer Solution (SIS) problem is as hard as solving some believed-to-be-difficult problem for every lattice. NTRU [HPS98] was an efficient public-key cryptosystem based on a new, potentially-hard problem over polynomial rings. Ajtai's line had strong guarantees but impractical instantiations; NTRU was practical but lacked a theoretical underpinning, and several NTRU-style constructions were later weakened or broken.

The two threads were woven together in the next decade: [Mic02, PR06, LM06] distilled the algebraic structure that gives lattice primitives NTRU-like efficiency with Ajtai-like guarantees, and Regev [Reg05] introduced the Learning with Errors (LWE) problem and showed that random LWE is as hard as (quantumly) solving worst-case lattice problems. The first fully-homomorphic encryption scheme [Gen09] and the threat of quantum computers [Sho97] added momentum; by the start of the NIST post-quantum process in 2017, lattice schemes were among the fastest and most compact quantum-resistant primitives. §1, p. 2

Scope and audience

The tutorial covers the mathematical concepts and design decisions behind the two main NIST lattice standards: the KEM / encryption scheme CRYSTALS-Kyber (ML-KEM) and the signature scheme CRYSTALS-Dilithium (ML-DSA), plus the ideas behind Frodo and NTRU. It assumes familiarity with indistinguishability, the hybrid argument, CPA-secure encryption, random oracles and the Fiat–Shamir transform; keeping ElGamal and Schnorr in mind helps. §1, pp. 2–3

Lattice cryptography is “messy”: security depends on several parameters, and there are many optimization tricks (dropping low-order bits, rounding). But it needs very few “deep” mathematical concepts. The advice is to work through the details with pencil and paper once, then keep the high-level ideas. §1, p. 3

Not covered: advanced constructions beyond encryption and signatures, precise cryptanalysis, the geometric side needed for FALCON and trapdoor sampling [GPV08, Pei10, MP12], and quantum aspects (the ROM versus the QROM). §1, pp. 3–4

§2Encryption

We start with encryption and the modest goal of CPA security: an eavesdropper who picks two messages cannot tell which one we encrypted. Active attackers wait for §4.8 §2, p. 5.

The route: write ElGamal with matrices and watch it break (§2.2), repair it with a little noise (§2.3–2.3.2), shrink the keys and ciphertexts (§2.4–2.5), and finish with key exchange (§2.6).

One thing will feel odd after ElGamal. Noise is what will protect the key, and noise can also, very rarely, make an honest ciphertext decrypt to the wrong message. We make that so unlikely that it does not appear to hurt security in practice, as long as the later proofs account for it §2, p. 5; footnote 5.

The encryption schemes of this section are most efficient when they allow a tiny decryption error (say, around $2^{-150}$). §2, p. 5

The paper’s exact statement

We start with a CPA-secure public-key encryption scheme: three algorithms (key generation, encryption, decryption), and CPA security means that for any two messages of the adversary's choosing, their encryptions are computationally indistinguishable. Generic transformations such as Fujisaki–Okamoto [FO99] later turn CPA security into CCA security (§4.8).

Unlike their discrete-log and factoring analogues, the paper’s schemes (“in this chapter”) are most efficient when they allow decryption errors: even when everything is run correctly, a ciphertext may, with very small probability, decrypt wrongly. An error around $2^{-150}$ does not appear to hurt practical security, as long as the transformations' proofs account for it. §2, p. 5; footnote 5

2.1Some Notation

The whole section turns on one word, small. Arithmetic is modulo $q$, and a number is small when it is close to 0 once we read it centred: with $q = 97$, the number 94 is really $-3$ (this page’s convention).

A short vector has only small entries. Secrets and errors will be short; public keys and ciphertexts will look like arbitrary numbers mod $q$.

$[\beta] = \{-\beta,\ldots,\beta\}$ holds the small numbers; $\lceil x\rfloor$ rounds, ties up. §2.1, Eq. (1)

The paper’s exact statement

All operations are in the ring $(\mathbb{Z}_q, +, \times)$. $a \leftarrow S$ means $a$ is chosen uniformly from the set $S$. For a positive integer $\beta$,

$$[\beta] = \{-\beta, \ldots, -1, 0, 1, \ldots, \beta\}. \qquad (1)$$

$[\beta]^{n\times m}$ is an $n\times m$ matrix with coefficients in $[\beta]$; vectors are column vectors; $\mathbf{v} \in [\beta]^m$ can also be written $\|\mathbf{v}\|_\infty \le \beta$. $\lceil x \rfloor$ is the closest integer to $x$, with ties broken upwards. For polynomials $a = \sum a_i X^i$, $a \leftarrow [\beta]$ means every coefficient is uniform in $[\beta]$; a distribution $\psi$ can replace $[\beta]$. §2.1, Eq. (1)

2.2A Motivating Example

Take ElGamal and change one thing: where it raises to a power, multiply by a matrix. The secret is a short vector $\mathbf{s}$, the public key a random matrix $\mathbf{A}$ with $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$, and every line of ElGamal gets a twin (table below).

Decryption works for ElGamal’s reason: sender and receiver each reach the same value from their own side, here $\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$, and it cancels. Matrices don’t commute, so $\mathbf{r}$ multiplies from the left and $\mathbf{s}$ from the right §2.2, footnote 6.

For security, $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})$ would have to look random to any efficient algorithm. It doesn’t.

The assumption is “clearly false”: Gaussian elimination recovers $\mathbf{s}$. A slightly modified version founds most lattice cryptography. §2.2, Eqs. (2)–(5)

The paper’s exact statement

Pretend that for $n \ge m$ and $\beta \ll q$ the distributions $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s})$ with $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^{n\times m}$, $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow [\beta]^m$, and $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{u})$ with $\mathbf{u} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$, are indistinguishable. This assumption is clearly false: Gaussian elimination inverts $\mathbf{A}$ (or an $m\times m$ sub-matrix) and checks whether some $\mathbf{s} \in [\beta]^m$ satisfies $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{u}$. But it gives an ElGamal-like scheme, with $\mu \in \mathbb{Z}_q$: §2.2

$$\mathsf{sk}: \mathbf{s} \leftarrow [\beta]^m,\quad \mathsf{pk}: (\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^{m\times m},\; \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}) \qquad (2)$$ $$(\mathbf{u}^T = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A},\; v = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{t} + \mu), \quad \mathbf{r} \leftarrow [\beta]^m \qquad (3)$$ $$\mu = v - \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s} \qquad (4)$$ $$v - \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{t} + \mu - \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mu - \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} = \mu. \qquad (5)$$

Security would follow from the assumption used twice: once for the public key, once (with $n$ and $m$ swapped) for $(\mathbf{A}', \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}')$ with $\mathbf{A}' = [\mathbf{A} \mid \mathbf{t}]$. If $m \gg n$ the public key could be statistically uniform (§2.5.5), but the ciphertext would still need the assumption. §2.2, Eqs. (2)–(5)

2.3The LWE Problem

The repair is almost embarrassingly small. Add a small error $\mathbf{e}$: publish $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}$, with $\mathbf{e}$ as short as $\mathbf{s}$ and just as secret.

Why would errors of size 1 stop elimination? Elimination computes $\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{s} + \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{e}$, and the entries of $\mathbf{A}^{-1}$ are typically nowhere near small, so the short $\mathbf{e}$ is scattered all over $\mathbb{Z}_q$. Switch on the noise in the elimination figure above: the same steps now land on $(-40, 35, -35, -34)$, far from $\mathbf{s}$. (Our toy and our reading; the paper says only that the error makes the attack “inapplicable”, §2.3, p. 7.)

$\mathsf{LWE}_{n,m,q,\beta}$: tell $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e})$ from $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{u})$, with $\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e}$ short and $\mathbf{u}$ uniform. §2.3, Definition 1

The paper’s exact statement

Definition 1 (LWE)

For positive integers $m, n, q$ and $\beta < q$, the $\mathsf{LWE}_{n,m,q,\beta}$ problem asks to distinguish

  1. $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e})$, where $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^{n\times m}$, $\mathbf{s} \leftarrow [\beta]^m$, $\mathbf{e} \leftarrow [\beta]^n$, from
  2. $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{u})$, where $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^{n\times m}$ and $\mathbf{u} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^n$.

The error $\mathbf{e}$ is what makes Gaussian elimination inapplicable. §2.3, Definition 1

Nobody proves this: the paper calls the adjusted assumption “plausibly valid” §2.3, p. 6, and §3 weighs the evidence.

It gets harder as $m$ and $\beta/q$ grow; $n$ barely matters, so we write $\mathsf{LWE}_{m,q,\beta}$. §2.3, p. 7

The paper’s exact statement

The problem becomes harder as $m$ and $\beta/q$ grow; $n$ has no known large impact unless it is as large as about $m^{2\beta+1}$ (linearization [AG11]), so we sometimes write $\mathsf{LWE}_{m,q,\beta}$. §2.3, p. 7

Uniform errors are for illustration: Regev's worst-case reductions [Reg09, Pei09] used rounded Gaussians, but this is not strictly necessary [DM13, MP13], and Kyber uses a binomial distribution because it is faster to sample. Definition 2 ($\mathsf{LWE}_{n,m,q,\psi}$) replaces $[\beta]$ by any distribution $\psi$. Some parameters make LWE trivially hard but useless (if $n < m$ and $\beta$ is large, $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e})$ can be statistically close to uniform), and some make it easy (if $m = 1$ it is not difficult to see whether $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e}$ is close to a multiple of the vector $\mathbf{A}$). Taking $\mathbf{s}$ from the same distribution as $\mathbf{e}$ gives an essentially equally hard problem [ACPS09]. §2.3, Definition 2, p. 7

Distinguishing $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{s}^T\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{e}^T)$ is also LWE, with $n$ and $m$ interchanged; by a hybrid argument, many samples $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{e}_i$ and $\mathbf{s}_j'^T\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{e}_j'^T$ are indistinguishable from uniform, losing a factor of at most $(t + t')$. §2.3, p. 8

2.3.1An LWE-Based Encryption Scheme

Decryption survives the noise, because attacker and receiver do very different things with it.

Elimination has to undo $\mathbf{A}$, and that scatters the error. The receiver undoes nothing. Subtracting $\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s}$ cancels the one big term, $\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$. What is left is $\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{e}_1 + e_3 - \mathbf{e}_2^T\mathbf{s}$: $2m+1$ terms, each a small number or a product of two.

Small, but not zero, so the message can no longer be any number mod $q$: the noise would blur it. We send one bit: 0 as 0 and 1 as $\lceil q/2\rfloor$, as far apart as the circle allows. The receiver asks which of the two the result is nearer.

The key and both ciphertext parts each get a small error, the bit becomes $0$ or $\lceil q/2\rfloor$, and security uses LWE twice. §2.3.1, Eqs. (6)–(7)

The paper’s exact statement

The message becomes a bit $\mu \in \{0,1\}$, and every product gets an error term:

$$\mathsf{sk}: \mathbf{s} \leftarrow [\beta]^m,\quad \mathsf{pk}: (\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_q^{m\times m},\; \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}_1),\; \mathbf{e}_1 \leftarrow [\beta]^m \qquad (6)$$ $$\Bigl(\mathbf{u}^T = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{e}_2^T,\;\; v = \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{t} + e_3 + \bigl\lceil \tfrac q2 \bigr\rfloor \mu\Bigr),\quad \mathbf{r}, \mathbf{e}_2 \leftarrow [\beta]^m,\; e_3 \leftarrow [\beta] \qquad (7)$$

$\lceil q/2 \rfloor$ is the element of $\mathbb{Z}_q$ closest to the rational $q/2$ (e.g. if $q = 13$, then $\lceil q/2 \rfloor = 7$). Security: $(\mathbf{A}, \mathbf{t})$ is indistinguishable from uniform by $\mathsf{LWE}_{m,q,\beta}$; then, with $\mathbf{A}' = [\mathbf{A} \mid \mathbf{t}]$, so is $(\mathbf{A}', \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A}' + [\mathbf{e}_2^T \mid e_3])$. LWE is used twice, with $m$ as the number of columns and then as the number of rows. §2.3.1, Eqs. (6)–(7), p. 8

Decryption leaves the message point plus noise of size at most $2m\beta^2+\beta$; if that is below $q/4$, the bit comes out right. §2.3.1, Eqs. (8)–(9)

The paper’s exact statement

To decrypt, one computes $v - \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s}$; instead of cleanly giving $\mu$ as in (4), it gives

$$v - \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s} = \mathbf{r}^T(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}_1) + e_3 + \tfrac q2\mu - (\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{e}_2^T)\mathbf{s} \qquad (8)$$ $$= \mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{e}_1 + e_3 + \tfrac q2\mu - \mathbf{e}_2^T\mathbf{s} \qquad (9)$$

The error terms have coefficients bounded by $\pm\beta$, so $\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{e}_1$ and $\mathbf{e}_2^T\mathbf{s}$ each consist of $m$ terms of magnitude at most $\beta^2$: the error lies in $[2m\beta^2 + \beta]$. If $2m\beta^2 + \beta < q/4$, the decryptor recovers $\mu$ by checking whether $v - \mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{s}$ is closer to $0$ or to $q/2$. §2.3.1, p. 9

2.3.2Bounding the Total Error, Exactly

The worst case needs every one of the $2m+1$ error terms to push the same way at full strength. That is quite unlikely.

The terms are random and centred on 0, so most of them cancel, roughly like a long random walk that ends far closer to home than its number of steps (an analogy). The noise is of order $\sqrt m\,\beta^2$, far below the worst case $2m\beta^2+\beta$ §2.3.2, p. 9.

We don’t need “never fails”, only “fails with probability around $2^{-150}$” (§2), so we want the exact tail. Write each random term as a polynomial whose coefficient of $X^i$ is the chance of the value $i$. Adding two independent terms then multiplies their polynomials.

The noise is a sum of independent terms, so its exact distribution is a product of polynomials. §2.3.2, Eqs. (10)–(12)

The paper’s exact statement

$2m\beta^2 + \beta$ is an upper bound, but the coefficients are random and centred, so the error is closer to $O(\sqrt m\,\beta^2)$. We want a bound that holds with very high probability (say $1 - 2^{-150}$), and for concrete parameters we can compute exactly §2.3.2

$$\Pr_{\mathbf{s},\mathbf{r},\mathbf{e}_1,\mathbf{e}_2 \leftarrow [\beta]^m,\, e_3 \leftarrow [\beta]}\bigl[\mathbf{r}^T\mathbf{e}_1 + e_3 - \mathbf{e}_2^T\mathbf{s} \in [\alpha]\bigr] \qquad (10)$$

using the fact that distributions of sums of independent random variables are products of polynomials: with $A(X) = \sum A_i X^i$, $B(X) = \sum B_i X^i$ and $C(X) = A(X)B(X) = \sum C_i X^i$,

$$\Pr[A + B = i] = C_i, \qquad \Pr[A+B \in [\alpha]] = \sum_{i=-\alpha}^{\alpha} C_i. \qquad (11),(12)$$

2.4Public Key and Ciphertext Size Trade-offs

The scheme works, so let’s weigh it. At the paper’s sizes, $m \approx 700$ and $q \approx 2^{13}$, one ciphertext is 9,113 bits (ours, from the paper’s formula), and it carries a single bit.

Where do the bits go? Almost all into $\mathbf{u}$, $m$ numbers mod $q$ against one for $v$, and $\mathbf{u}$ involves only $\mathbf{A}$ and the sender’s randomness, not the receiver’s $\mathbf{t}$ or the message. So reuse it: give the receiver $\ell$ secrets, the columns of a matrix $\mathbf{S}$. One $\mathbf{u}$ then gives a different shared value with each secret, and each hides its own bit. With $k$ rows of randomness we encrypt $N = k\ell$ bits at once.

2.5.1Reducing the Ciphertext Size by Removing the Low-Order Part

After amortizing, $\mathbf{V}$ still spends $\log q$ bits on every message bit: about 13 bits to carry 1 at the paper’s $q \approx 2^{13}$. Do we need all of them?

No, because of the noise we already live with. Decryption only asks whether a value is nearer 0 or $q/2$, so the low-order part of each entry of $\mathbf{V}$ is small change. Send only $\kappa$ bits per coefficient: the nearest of $2^\kappa$ evenly spread points on the circle.

The rounding is one more error, $\mathbf{E}'$, and a mild one: it enters decryption on its own, while the other error terms come from inner products, which are much larger. So $\kappa$ can usually be a small constant like 3 or 4 §2.5.1, p. 12.

With evenly spread points no value moves more than $\lceil q/2^{\kappa+1}\rceil$, and decryption gains one small error. §2.5.1, Eqs. (18)–(19), Figure 1

2.5.3Learning with Rounding

We now add error twice: once on purpose ($\mathbf{e}$, for security) and once as a side effect (rounding, for size). That looks redundant.

Sometimes it is. Round $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}$ to a coarse set of points and ask what $\mathbf{e}$ changed. Usually nothing: a nudge of at most $\beta$ matters only if $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$ was already within $\beta$ of a midpoint between two points. When $q$ is large next to $\beta$ and $|\mathcal{S}|$, the gaps are wide and that is rare.

Then $\mathbf{e}$ rarely changes the rounded value, so drop it: that is Learning with Rounding, at least as hard as LWE when $\mathbf{e}$ would not matter. §2.5.3, pp. 14–15

The paper’s exact statement

By LWE, $(\mathbf{A}, \mathrm{HIGH}_\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}))$ is indistinguishable from $(\mathbf{A}, \mathrm{HIGH}_\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{u}))$. 2.5.5LWE Encryption with a “Non-Square” Public Key

Every scheme so far uses LWE twice: public key and ciphertext both only look random. Some applications need a truly uniform public key, so LWE is used only once.

Counting gives us that. Suppose there are far more short secrets $\mathbf{s}$ than possible values of $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}$, that is, $(2\beta+1)^m \gg q^n$. Then the public key needs no error and no assumption (next line). $\mathbf{A}$ gets more columns than rows; hence “non-square”.

2.5.6Using Distinct Distributions for the Secret and the Error

We have drawn $\mathbf{s}$ and $\mathbf{e}$ from the same set all along. Nothing forced that.

For the best known algorithms, hardness depends on the norm of $(\mathbf{s}, \mathbf{e}_1)$, secret and error taken together §2.5.6, p. 16. But decryption noise is made of products, secret times error. So security looks at $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2$ and correctness at $\sigma_1\sigma_2$. With the sum of squares fixed, the product is largest when the two are equal, just as a rectangle with a fixed diagonal has the most area when it is a square. So we split the total unevenly.

2.6Non-Interactive Key Exchange (NIKE)

Encryption already gives key transport, but in a fixed order: nobody can encrypt before the public key arrives.

§3Hardness of LWE and Other Lattice Problems

Everything in §2 is safe only if LWE is hard, and all we have seen is Gaussian elimination failing. What better evidence is there?

The paper’s answer is a picture and a hard look at the best known attack. Behind them stands one theoretical remark: asymptotically, solving some problem on random lattices of our kind is as hard as solving some problem on any lattice p. 19. The picture is geometric. $\mathbf{A}$ draws a regular grid of points, a lattice. An LWE value $\mathbf{t}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e}$ marks a spot close to the grid, and at the sizes we use a random $\mathbf{t}$ almost always marks a spot far from it. Breaking LWE means telling the two apart.

The attack needs one tool, a short vector of a lattice. So we ask three things in turn: do short vectors exist, how well can today’s algorithms find them, and how short must one be to break LWE?

The geometric view is not needed to see how the schemes work, but it is crucial for their security. §3, p. 19

3.1Lattices

First we need the grid itself. What is a lattice, and which one does $\mathbf{A}$ define?

A lattice is a set of integer vectors that we can add and subtract without leaving the set. We can pin one down by building blocks, a basis, taking every integer combination of them; or by a test, keeping every vector that passes. The paper compares the two to a code’s generator and parity-check matrices. p. 19

Cryptography uses the test. A pair $(\mathbf{v}_1,\mathbf{v}_2)$ is in our lattice when $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{v}_1+\mathbf{v}_2\equiv\mathbf{0}$ mod $q$. A public key satisfies $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}+\mathbf{e}\equiv\mathbf{t}$: the same equation with $\mathbf{t}$ in place of $\mathbf{0}$. So the lattice is the set of pairs that would give the all-zero public key.

The $q$-ary lattice $\mathcal{L}_q^\perp([\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{I}_n])$ is the set of integer vectors that pass a parity check mod $q$, and Eq. (31) gives it a basis. §3.1, Eqs. (28)–(32), p. 19

3.1.1The Quotient Group and Determinant

The secret $(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{e})$ lives in a shifted copy of the lattice.

Slide the whole lattice by an integer vector $\mathbf{z}$: every point of the copy gives the same value under the test, $[\mathbf{A}\mid\mathbf{I}_n]\mathbf{z}$ mod $q$. That value is the copy’s label, and the copy is called a coset. One coset per label makes $q^n$ of them, and together they use every integer vector exactly once.

So only one integer vector in $q^n$ is a lattice point. That number is the determinant, and also the volume of the cell of any basis.

$\det\Lambda$ is one over the density of $\Lambda$. It equals $|\det\mathbf{B}|$ and the number of cosets: $q^n$ for the lattice of (31). §3.1.1, pp. 19–20

The paper’s exact statement

The determinant of a full-rank lattice $\Lambda \subseteq \mathbb{Z}^m$ is the inverse of its density: with $S_r = \{\mathbf{z} \in \mathbb{Z}^m : \|\mathbf{z}\| < r\}$, $$\det(\Lambda) = \lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{|S_r|}{|\Lambda \cap S_r|}.$$ If $\Lambda = \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{B})$ for a full-rank $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{Z}^{m\times m}$, then $\det(\Lambda) = |\det(\mathbf{B})|$; for the $(n+m)$-dimensional lattice in (31) this is $q^n$. 3.1.2Distance to the Lattice

An LWE coset contains a very short vector, the secret $(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{e})$. Does a random coset?

§4Encryption Over Polynomial Rings

What does a 256-bit key cost at safe sizes? The LWE encryption of §2.3.1 spends a whole ciphertext on a single bit.

The size calculator in §4.3.1 puts numbers on it (our arithmetic): at LWE dimension 768 with a 12-bit $q$, 256 bits sent one at a time take 295,296 bytes of ciphertext. §2.4 brings that down to 18,816, but only by growing the public key from 1,184 bytes to 18,464.

The fix is to change what a “number” is. Every entry of $\mathbf{A}$, of $\mathbf{s}$ and of the message stops being an integer modulo $q$ and becomes a polynomial with $d$ coefficients. A message polynomial holds $d$ bits, so one ciphertext carries the whole key and the public key has no need to grow.

Polynomials bring structure, which makes things fast (§4.6) and can also help an attacker (§4.5), so we go carefully. The destination is Kyber (§4.7).

4.1Polynomial Rings

Multiply two polynomials and the degrees add: two of degree 3 give one of degree 6. How do we keep them a fixed size?

As $\mathbb{Z}_q$ keeps integers a fixed size: after every multiplication, divide and keep the remainder. We divide by a fixed polynomial $f$ of degree $d$, so an element of the ring $\mathcal{R}_f$ is just $d$ integer coefficients, and adding two elements is adding two vectors of length $d$.

The division is the long division from school, and it never needs fractions, because $f$ is monic: its leading coefficient is 1.

For a monic $f$ of degree $d$, every $a$ is uniquely $a = bf + r$ with $\deg(r) < d$, and $a \bmod f = r$. §4.1, p. 26

The paper’s exact statement

$\mathbb{Z}[X]$ is the ring of polynomials $a = \sum_i a_i X^i$ with integer coefficients. $\deg(a)$ is the largest $i$ with $a_i \neq 0$; $a$ is monic if $a_{\deg(a)} = 1$; $a$ is irreducible if it cannot be written as $a = bc$ with $\deg(b), \deg(c) < \deg(a)$. §4.1

For a monic $f \in \mathbb{Z}[X]$ of degree $d$, the ring $\mathcal{R}_f$ (often written $\mathbb{Z}[X]/(f(X))$, footnote 12) consists of the polynomials $a = \sum_{i=0}^{d-1} a_i X^i$. Addition is coefficient-wise, so $\mathcal{R}_f$ under addition is $\mathbb{Z}^d$. Multiplication is ordinary multiplication followed by reduction modulo $f$: every $a$ can be written uniquely as $a = bf + r$ with $\deg(r) < d$, and $a \bmod f = r$. The usual $\mathbb{Z}$ is the special case $f = X$ (or $f = X - \alpha$). §4.1, p. 26–27

4.1.1Polynomials and Linear Algebra

Multiplying by a fixed polynomial is a matrix, which is a relief: LWE was all matrices and vectors, and so was every attack in §3.

The product $ab \bmod f$ is linear in $b$, so it is enough to know what $a$ does to $1, X, \dots, X^{d-1}$. Those are the columns: counting from 0, column $i$ is $aX^i \bmod f$ (Eqs. (42), (44)).

4.1.2Coefficient Growth

A bad $f$ can make small things large. §2 rested on small times small being small. With integers that is automatic; with polynomials it depends on $f$.

A product $ab$ is $\mathcal{M}_a\mathcal{V}_b$, so it is small when the entries of $\mathcal{M}_a$ are. For $f = X^d \pm 1$ the overflow returns as one coefficient, at worst with its sign flipped, so every entry of $\mathcal{M}_a$ is a coefficient of $a$ up to sign: the best we could hope for.

Now take $f = X^d + 2X^{d-1} + 1$. Here $X^d = -2X^{d-1} - 1$: each overflow comes back doubled, into the very slot that overflows next. The doubling compounds, and the entries of $\mathcal{M}_a$ grow exponentially in $d$. The paper’s verdict on such $f$: not useful for cryptographic purposes.

Only $X^d \pm 1$ give $\|\mathcal{M}_a\|_\infty = \|\mathcal{V}_a\|_\infty$; $X^d \pm X^{d/2} + 1$ and $\sum_{i=0}^{d} X^i$ give at most twice that; $X^d + 2X^{d-1} + 1$ grows exponentially. §4.1.2, p. 28

The paper’s exact statement

4.3Generalized-LWE Encryption

Encryption gains a bigger message. Take the scheme of §2.3.1 and change nothing but the type: every integer becomes an element of $\mathcal{R}_f$.

Before, $\mu$ was one bit, sent as $0$ or $q/2$. Now $\mu$ is a polynomial with $d$ coefficients, each 0 or 1, and every coefficient is its own small copy of the old scheme, blurred by its own noise. One ciphertext, $d$ bits.

Decryption is the same cancellation, and the small terms left over are now products of ring elements. This is where §4.1.2 pays off: with $f = X^d \pm 1$, the chance that one coefficient of the noise exceeds $q/4$ is computed just as it was over the integers, and we add up the $d$ chances (a union bound).

4.4NTRU

Polynomial rings entered lattice cryptography not through LWE but with NTRU, which hides its secret in a different place: a short detour.

NTRU is a trapdoor one-way function, closer in spirit to RSA than to ElGamal (our analogy, not the paper’s). Anyone can compute $(s, e) \mapsto as + e$ for the public ring element $a$ and small $s, e$, and the output has the same form as a ring-LWE sample. But $a$ is not random: the key holder built it from two small secret polynomials, and one of them, $g_2$, is the trapdoor that recovers $s$ and $e$.

4.4.1The NTRU Trapdoor 1-way Function

The trapdoor does modular arithmetic with two relatively prime moduli at once, which the paper notes “is very rarely an idea that leads to any meaningful results” (p. 31).

The public $a$ is $p\,g_1g_2^{-1}$, where $g_1, g_2$ are small secret polynomials and $p$ is a small integer ($p = 3$ when the coefficients are in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$). Multiply $b$ by the secret $g_2$. The $g_2^{-1}$ cancels: $g_2b = p\,g_1s + g_2e$ modulo $q$.

Everything on the right is built from small things, so if $q$ is chosen large enough, its coefficients stay below $q/2$ in magnitude: the reduction modulo $q$ does nothing, and the equation holds over the integers. So we may reduce it modulo $p$ instead. The first term is a multiple of $p$ and vanishes, leaving $g_2e$, and dividing by $g_2$ modulo $p$ gives $e$ modulo $p$. Since $p = 2\beta + 1$, that pins down $e$ exactly, and $s$ follows.

Here is a ring with exactly the kind of weakness §4.3.2 warned about.

Take $f = X^d - 1$, the ring NTRU first used. Put $X = 1$ and $f$ becomes $0$, so “evaluate at 1”, which just adds up the coefficients of a polynomial, survives reduction modulo $f$: sums go to sums, products to products. It maps the whole ring onto plain $\mathbb{Z}_q$.

Apply it to a ring instance $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e}$. Every ring element collapses to a single number and the equation survives: $\mathbf{A}'\mathbf{s}' + \mathbf{e}' = \mathbf{t}'$. The fatal part is that small stays small: $d$ coefficients from $[\beta]$ add up to at most $d\beta$ in magnitude. The attacker now holds an integer LWE instance with $m$ columns instead of $dm$, its secrets in $[d\beta]$: a small-dimensional problem, solvable as in §3.3 (p. 32).

The hardness reductions the paper cites ask only that $f$ be irreducible over the integers (for SIS, a large-degree irreducible factor is enough); nothing is asked modulo $q$.

4.6Exploiting the Algebraic Structure … for Efficiency (The NTT)

Structure can also buy speed. The costliest operation in the scheme is multiplying polynomials, and the schoolbook way takes $d^2$ products: 65,536 for Kyber’s $d = 256$ (our arithmetic).

We factor $f$ modulo $q$ only, which the reductions do not forbid, and compute in the pieces. If $X^d + 1 \equiv (X^{d/2} - r)(X^{d/2} + r) \pmod q$, the Chinese Remainder Theorem says that a polynomial modulo $X^d + 1$ carries the same information as its two remainders modulo the halves. So we reduce $a$ and $b$ modulo each half, multiply half by half, and glue the results back together.

Reducing modulo $X^{d/2} - r$ is cheap: replace $X^{d/2}$ by $r$, which folds the top half of the coefficients onto the bottom half. By itself that buys little: two half-size schoolbook products are still quadratic. But if the halves split again and again, we recurse down to single numbers. That is $\log d$ levels of about $d$ operations each, $O(d \log d)$ in all.

4.8From CPA Encryption to a CCA-KEM

Kyber as built so far is CPA-secure: safe against someone who only listens. But a server decrypts whatever arrives.

One move shows the danger (our illustration, not the paper’s). An LWE ciphertext is malleable, as an ElGamal ciphertext is: add about $q/2$ to one coefficient of $v$ and the matching message bit flips. CCA security asks that the shared key still look random even to an adversary with access to a decapsulation oracle (on any ciphertext but the challenge).

The Fujisaki–Okamoto transform makes that oracle useless: it should answer only for ciphertexts whose message the attacker already knows. The sender hashes a random $m$ with the public key. One half of the hash is the shared key $K$, the other the coins $\rho$ used to encrypt $m$, so each $m$ has exactly one valid ciphertext. The receiver decrypts to $m'$, encrypts it again in the same way, and compares.

§5Digital Signatures from Σ-Protocols

The same rings can sign, and the road is Schnorr’s.

Schnorr’s public key is $h = g^x$, and a signature is a proof that the signer knows $x$, given without handing $x$ over. It takes two steps: a three-move conversation (commit, challenge, respond), then a hash in place of the person asking the challenge. We walk the same road from lattices, with $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{s}_1 + \mathbf{s}_2$ in place of $g^x$.

Two things go wrong on the way, both because our secret is small. Smallness is awkward to prove, and a small secret shows through the answers the prover gives. Repairing those takes §5.1 and §5.2. §5.3 is a side trip that ends in a toy signature of our own, and the rest of the section shrinks the result until it is Dilithium.

The paper’s exact statement

5.2The Basic Σ-Protocol

We copy Schnorr move for move and see what breaks.

Schnorr’s prover picks a random mask $y$, sends $w = g^y$, receives a challenge $c$ and answers $z = y + xc$. Copy it with vectors: masks $\mathbf{y}_1, \mathbf{y}_2$ and answers $\mathbf{z}_i = c\mathbf{s}_i + \mathbf{y}_i$.

The answers must stay small, because the short witness of §5.1 will be built from them, so the masks must come from a bounded range. But then the possible answers are that range slid sideways by $c\mathbf{s}$, and an answer near the edge gives the slide away. With the figure’s toy numbers: masks go up to 6, so an answer of 7 can only mean $cs \ge 1$.

The fix is blunt. Draw the masks from a range a little wider than the answers may have, and send an answer only when it lands in a middle window $[\bar\beta]$ that every possible slide still covers. Otherwise send $\perp$ and start again. The paper calls this rejection sampling.

5.2.1Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge

The window really hides the secret, and the proof is a count.

Take one coefficient and fix the secret’s slide, some $\nu_s$ in $[\gamma]$. A given window value $\nu_z$ comes from exactly one mask, $\nu_y = \nu_z - \nu_s$, and the mask range $[\bar\beta + \gamma]$ always contains it. So sent answers are uniform on the window, and the chance of landing in it is the same for every secret. In the toy above: 9 window values out of 13 masks, for each of the five slides.

So anyone can write a convincing conversation without the key: choose the answers and the challenge first, then solve for the commitment, as in Schnorr’s proof. A conversation we could have written ourselves teaches us nothing. This covers conversations that end in an answer; the signer never shows the others (§5.6).

5.2.2Proof of Knowledge

A convincing prover must know something. The argument is Schnorr’s, and it is a thought experiment, not a move of the protocol.

Take any prover that manages to convince us. Let it send its commitment $\mathbf{w}$; then, like rewinding a tape, put it back at that point and ask a different challenge. If it answers both, we subtract one verification equation from the other and $\mathbf{w}$ cancels, leaving an equation between the differences of the answers and of the challenges.

In Schnorr that equation is $g^{\bar z} = h^{\bar c}$, and dividing $\bar z$ by $\bar c$ gives the secret $x$ itself. Here dividing would not help, since nothing says the quotient is small (our remark). So we leave $\bar c$ in the equation: the relaxed statement of §5.1. Differences of two answers reach $2\bar\beta$, and the difference of two challenges lies in $[2]$. In the figure’s first run the differences are $(4, -2)$ with $\bar c = -2$: that is $\bar c$ times the secret $(-2, 1)$ illustration.

5.2.4Setting the Parameters

Two problems now guard the scheme, at two very different sizes.

Ring-LWE guards the key, whose secrets have coefficients in $[\beta]$. Ring-SIS guards against forgery, where the witness pulled out by rewinding has coefficients up to $2\bar\beta$. And $\bar\beta$ is not ours to pick freely: the window must be wide compared with the slide $\gamma = \eta\beta$ times the number of coefficients, $d(m+n)$, or the prover almost never answers (next line). So the forger’s target is far looser than the key’s secret.

5.3Analogies to Discrete-Log Schemes: Schnorr, Okamoto, and Katz–Wang

Other sizes give Schnorr’s cousins. The paper marks this side trip as skippable (footnote 24); we need only the stop we add at its end, a toy signature of our own.

Schnorr has two well-known discrete-log cousins: Okamoto’s, where one public key has many matching secret keys, and Katz–Wang’s, whose security proof needs no rewinding. The lattice protocol of §5.2 turns into either by moving one dial. Make $\beta$ large, and many small secrets fit the same key: Okamoto-like. Make $\bar\beta$ so small that, for a random key, answers to two different challenges cannot both exist (with high probability): Katz–Wang-like. Both pay in LWE or SIS hardness (next line) and remain, the paper says, “mostly of only theoretical interest”.

Okamoto-like needs $\beta > q^{n/(n+m)}$ and Katz–Wang-like $\bar\beta < q^{n/(n+m)}$; both put $\beta$ and $\bar\beta$ on one side of the LWE/SIS crossing, so the unconstrained Schnorr-like one is the most efficient (Figure 7). §5.3, Figure 7, pp. 45–49

Schnorr

Public key $g$, $h = g^x$. The prover sends $w = g^y$ and answers $c$ with $z = y + xc$; the verifier checks $g^z = h^c\cdot w$. To reduce from discrete log, use the challenge $(g, h)$ as the public key. Without $x$, simulate by picking random $z, c$ and setting $w = g^z/h^c$. Okamoto

Okamoto [Oka92] can be proven secure against active adversaries, who choose challenges maliciously. This is not really needed for signatures, since Fiat–Shamir only needs HVZK (footnote 25: there is no reduction from discrete log or DDH to actively secure Schnorr, though it can be proven under “knowledge assumptions” [BP02]). The public key is $(g_1, g_2)$, $h = g_1^{x_1}g_2^{x_2}$. The prover sends $w = g_1^{y_1}g_2^{y_2}$ and answers with $z_i = y_i + cx_i$; the verifier checks $g_1^{z_1}g_2^{z_2} = h^c w$.

The reduction picks its own valid secret key and runs the protocol honestly, with no simulation. Rewinding an impersonator gives $h^{\bar c} = g_1^{\bar z_1}g_2^{\bar z_2}$, i.e. $1 = g_1^{\bar z_1 - x_1\bar c}g_2^{\bar z_2 - x_2\bar c}$ (Eqs. (82)–(83)). This yields $\log_{g_1} g_2$ unless both exponents are $0$. Many secret keys are consistent with $h$, and the transcripts are identically distributed for all of them (footnote 26), even with adversarial $c$. So even an unbounded impersonator hits exactly $\bar z_i = x_i\bar c$ only with small probability. (This fails for Schnorr: $h = g^x$ has only one secret key.)

Lattice analogue: choose $\beta$ so large that the public key does not determine the secret. Katz–Wang

Katz–Wang [KW03, Section 3] rests entirely on DDH and needs no rewinding, which gives a tighter reduction (footnote 28). But it does not seem to affect actual security, so Okamoto, Katz–Wang and their lattice versions “remain mostly of only theoretical interest.” The public key is $(g_1, g_2)$, $(h_1 = g_1^x, h_2 = g_2^x)$. 5.4Reducing the Proof Size

The small part, $\mathbf{z}_2$, can go.

In the verifier’s equation $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z}_1 + \mathbf{z}_2 - c\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{w}$, $\mathbf{z}_2$ is a small correction to large numbers, roughly like the last digits of a long number (an analogy). Change them a little and the leading digits stay put, unless the number sat right at a rounding boundary.

So let the commitment be only the leading part, $\mathbf{w} = \mathrm{HIGH}_\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y})$: the nearest point of a coarse grid $\mathcal{S}$, as in §2.5.1. The verifier computes $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} - c\mathbf{t}$, which equals $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y} - c\mathbf{s}_2$: the right number, off by a slide of at most $\gamma$. Unless that number is within $\gamma$ of a rounding boundary, its leading part is $\mathbf{w}$, and the verifier never needs $\mathbf{z}_2$.

5.6Digital Signatures

Some optimizations in ML-DSA

Hash the message once, sample $\mathbf{y}$ with exactly 20 bits per coefficient, and send only the positions of the hint’s ones: about 3290 bytes. §5.7, p. 57

The paper’s exact statement
  • Hash the message once. If $\mu'$ is long, recomputing $\mathcal H(\mathrm{HIGH}_\mathcal{S}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}), \mu, \mathbf{A}, \mathbf{t})$ after every restart is wasteful. First hash $\mu'$ with SHA-512, together with the public key, into a 512-bit digest $\mu$.
  • Power-of-two masking range. $[\gamma + \bar\beta] = [2^{19} - 1]$ has $2^{20} - 1$ values; sampling instead from $\{-(2^{19}-1), \ldots, 2^{19}-1, 2^{19}\}$ uses exactly 20 random bits.
  • Compact hint. With high probability $\mathbf{h}$ has at most 55 ones. Send their positions (8 bits each, $8\cdot55$ bits) plus the boundaries between the 6 polynomials ($5\cdot6 = 30$ bits): 470 bits instead of 1536. The signature drops to about 3290 bytes. The signer restarts if there are more than 55 ones, which is experimentally very rare.

§5.7, p. 57

Note on the paper's text

The paper says to specify “the positions of the 0's” and a “naive $256\cdot8 = 1536$”-bit string. The positions sent are those of the non-zero hint bits, and $1536 = 256\cdot6$.

What to remember from §5

  1. Schnorr’s road map: mask, commit, challenge, respond $\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{y} + c\mathbf{s}$, then Fiat–Shamir.
  2. Prove a relaxed statement; by Lemma 9 that still means Ring-LWE or Ring-SIS.
  3. Rejection sampling makes sent answers uniform whatever the secret (Lemma 10).
  4. High bits and hints drop $\mathbf{z}_2$ from the signature and the low bits of $\mathbf{t}$ from the key.
  5. Dilithium: about 5 attempts, a 1952-byte key, about 3290-byte signatures with the compact hint (§5.7, pp. 56–57).

So, can we rebuild them?

§1 asked whether we could rebuild ElGamal and Schnorr from noisy linear algebra, and why anyone would believe the result is hard to break. We can: keep the shape of the discrete-log scheme and replace $g^x$ by a public $\mathbf{A}$ times a small secret, plus a small error.

Kyber is ElGamal rebuilt this way. The small error defeats Gaussian elimination (§2). Polynomials let one ciphertext carry a whole key without a bigger public key, and the NTT makes them fast (§4). The Fujisaki–Okamoto transform protects against chosen ciphertexts.

Dilithium is Schnorr rebuilt this way: answer with $\mathbf{z} = c\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{y}$, refuse whenever the answer would give the secret away, and let a hash ask the challenge (§5).

And why believe it is hard? Both rest on LWE over polynomial rings, Dilithium on SIS as well, and §3 read both as lattice problems: SIS asks for a short vector, LWE for telling points close to a lattice from random ones, and the attack §3 weighs against each is a search for short vectors. The sizes are chosen so that the best known attacks fall short: evidence, not a proof (our caution). The details are messy, as §1 warned, but the deep ideas are few (§1, p. 3). We have just rebuilt them.
