Anduin

Rating providers for AI agents from the payments they already make.

AI agents buy services from each other. They pay for each request, often one cent at a time. The payments settle in stablecoins on a public blockchain. The median payment is \$0.01.

Anyone can list a service. An agent cannot tell a good provider from a fraud.

Every known fix taxes the market: a curator, credentials, a paid referee, or reviews nobody writes. Anduin adds none of them. It reads only the payments that already settled, and it makes a high rating cost real money. Each step below has one control, and every number on it comes from the paper.

Step 1  The market

An agent must choose between strangers

A buyer needs a service it never bought. It sees endpoints, and the descriptions their owners wrote. There are no brands and no reviews. Which stranger does it pay?

Each proposed fix changes the market. Select one to see its shape.

    Step 2  Drawing the market

    The ledger is already a graph

    Blockchain payments are public and permanent. Anyone can read who paid whom, and how much. Buyers pay providers. Providers never pay providers. So the record sorts into two columns, and the paper calls this a bipartite graph.

    One edge is one buyer's whole relationship with one provider. Its weight is the sum of their payments in the window, fees included. A thicker edge carries more dollars. This graph is the only input.

    Two sets matter, and they are marked below. $K(b)$ holds the providers the buyer already pays. $C(b)$ holds the other buyers that pay those same providers. Step 3 uses nothing else.

    Hover or tap an edge to read its weight. The weights come from Figure 4 of the paper. The rest of the market is texture and carries no values.

    Step 3  Scoring a stranger

    Trust moves along a path. Its thinnest edge stops it.

    Buyer $b$ pays two providers. Those two are its known providers, the set $K(b)$. Other buyers pay the same providers. They are co-users, the set $C(b)$; this page calls them customers. Both customers also pay $p'$, which $b$ never paid. $p'$ is the candidate.

    A customer that pays your providers needs what you need. Its next choice is evidence. That evidence moves from $b$ to a customer through the providers they share. Each shared provider carries no more than the thinner of its two edges. Add those up and you get the reach to that customer. The customer's own payment to the candidate then caps what arrives.

    Move the sliders. These two reaches are what every Anduin rating is built from.

    Step 4  The attack

    One provider, many fake identities

    A provider wants first place. Entry is free, so it creates its own buyer identities. One party posing as many is the Sybil attack, named in 2002.

    One asymmetry carries the whole defence. A payment to the attacker's own provider costs only the fee, about \$0.017, because the money returns. A payment to a provider it does not own costs the full amount, and that money is gone. And no attacker can create a payment from the honest buyer.

    Step 5  Anduin

    Make trust a quantity that flows

    The rule: trust reaches a candidate only on paths that start at the buyer's own payments, and a path carries no more than its thinnest edge. A fake that paid a known provider one cent carries one cent. More fakes do not help.

    Three constructions apply the rule. They differ in how much of the buyer's pipes each customer may use. The sliders from Step 3 drive all three, and the page solves the real max-flow for each one.

    Amplified flow widens each buyer pipe by $n_p$. That count is per provider: how many customers pay that provider and the candidate. Known provider 1 has two, so its pipe becomes 2 × \$4. Known provider 2 has only one, so its pipe becomes 1 × \$1.

    Why more fakes buy nothing

    The paper proves two bounds. The first: if the attacker pays $m$ dollars to providers it does not own, no Anduin rating scores it above $m$. The identity count does not matter, and the bound is tight. So its only lever is money it gives away. Split a budget across more fakes and the score holds still while the fee bill climbs.

    The second bound is stricter, and covers maximum flow alone. Its score never exceeds the buyer's own spend, whatever the attacker's budget. Step 3 shows that ceiling.

    Step 6  Results

    What it does on real money

    The test. 157 days of real agent payments through x402, which carries most machine payment volume: \$6.1M against roughly \$270K for the next rail. Every rating reads the 90 days up to June 21, 2026. 136,646 buyers were active. 3,656 paid again within 30 days. 813 of those paid a provider they had never paid before. Those 813 are the test set, and each sees 381.4 candidates on average. Did the rating put the right provider first?

    Step 7  Shipping it

    What you get, and what you accept

    Four things to know before you ship it. Two are proved. Two are awkward.

    • Proved

      A score costs money, not identities. Pay $m$ dollars to providers you do not control, and no Anduin rating scores you above $m$. Any number of fakes gives the same result. The bound is tight, so that is the real price.

    • Proved

      Maximum flow is capped by the buyer. Its score never exceeds what that buyer has spent with its own known providers. The attacker's budget does not change this.

    • Nothing to change

      Nothing has to change. The input is payments that already settled. No party must agree a convention before it transacts. Every other proposal needs that agreement.

      The cost to compute is local. Summation flow precomputes one value per customer, then answers each candidate in constant time. Maximum and amplified flow solve one small max-flow per candidate, over the buyer's own neighbourhood and never the whole market.

    • Live with it

      Cold start is real. A new buyer has no known providers, no customers and no candidates, so every rating is undefined. Collaborative filtering has the same hole. One settled payment starts it. A new provider stays unrated until one customer pays it.

      Shared wallets blur. One address can be the treasury for several agents. Their histories pool into one node, and the ratings get noisier. Per-agent subaccount keys — ERC-4337 session keys, BIP-32 derivation — would split them again.

    Ship amplified flow. It has the best accuracy of the three, and the highest attack cost of all six at rank 1. Avoid maximum flow in a product: its guarantee is the strongest, but it ties at the top for 78.0% of buyers, so it cannot name a first pick.

    Next  Future work

    How a brand-new buyer gets its first ranking

    Cold start is the open problem, and it bites the buyer first. A buyer that has never paid anyone has no known providers. With $K(b)$ empty there are no customers and no candidates, so every rating is undefined. An undefined rating cannot be ranked.

    One way out: publish a small curated set of trusted providers. A new buyer credits each one a fixed amount — say \$1 — and those artificial edges become its $K(b)$. Nothing else is invented. The real customers of those providers become $C(b)$, the providers they pay become the candidates, and Anduin ranks them exactly as it would for any other buyer.

    The seed size is the whole trade. It is also the ceiling: maximum flow can never score any candidate above what the buyer has spent, and at the start that is only the seed. So a small seed gives a weak but honest ranking that real payments quickly outweigh. A large seed hands the curated set the ranking, which is the gatekeeper Anduin set out to remove.

The questions we would test next: how large a seed can be before the attack cost falls, and how fast real payments outweigh it.
