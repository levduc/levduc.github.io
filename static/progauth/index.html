Progressive Authentication

A Pointwise Soundness Guarantee for Interruptible Verifiers

If a verifier is stopped after $t$ of its checks, what has it proven? We make that a precise, adversarially robust number: a confidence $\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t)$ for every prefix of the verification. We work it out for four verifiers. For Fiat–Shamir proofs it holds a surprise: the first checks can prove nothing at all.

Circle Research, USA

1 · The puzzle

Seventy FRI queries passed. What did they prove?

A STARK proof is checked by FRI queries, each an independent spot check of the proof. At ethSTARK's provable 128-bit design point a proof carries $s = 140$ of them. Suppose your verifier is interrupted after the first 70, and every one of them passed. The prover could compute $Q = 2^{80}$ hashes. How much soundness has the verifier proven so far?

The 140 queries of one proof, in the order they are checked

    Bits of soundness proven after $t$ queries

    Your guess, at $t = 70$:

    Half the checks passed, and they proved nothing. A Fiat–Shamir prover derives its own challenges by hashing, so it can retry until a short prefix of queries passes. Full verification hides that retry budget inside all 140 queries. Interruption exposes it. The rest of this page builds the definition that says exactly when a prefix starts to count.

    The parameters are those the paper uses for ethSTARK's provable 128-bit point (§5.2): rate $\rho = 1/4$, proximity slack $m = 3$, $s = 140$ queries, a $w = 20$-bit proof-of-work, against $Q = 2^{80}$ random-oracle queries, for a word $\delta$-far from the code with $\delta \ge 5/12$, where $\alpha_\circ = 7/12$. A bit of soundness here is $-\log_2$ of the bound on accepting a false statement. The 48.9 bits at $t = 140$ is the query term $2^{-48.9}$, which the paper calls ethSTARK's 128-bit level against $Q = 2^{80}$ (§5.2). The figures hold in the provable proximity regime of Ben-Sasson et al. (BCIKS) and neglect $\varepsilon_c(Q)$, the commit-phase and compilation terms, as the paper's estimates do. Figure 3(a) of the paper shows the same split schematically; this grid of 140 cells is our drawing.

    What this page shows

    Claim 1

    Confidence is a function of how far verification got. A progressive verifier reports a proven bound after every step $t$. The bound holds at each $t$ separately, jointly with the stopping point $t^{*} = t$, so it stays valid against a prover that stops at its longest passing prefix; no union over the stopping point is needed (Def. 7; Appendix B of the paper).

    Claim 2

    Under Fiat–Shamir, the first steps can prove nothing. At ethSTARK's provable 128-bit point, the first 77 of 140 FRI queries carry no provable soundness against $Q = 2^{80}$ (Thm. 2 and §5.2, in the BCIKS regime, neglecting $\varepsilon_c(Q)$).

    The checks must run in derivation order. A batched multi-opening checks the $t$ smallest sampled positions instead.     2 · Why did that happen?

    A verifier that can stop, and a prover that can retry

    We first need words for a verifier that can be stopped. In a progressive authentication scheme (Def. 1), $\mathtt{Verify}$ is iterable: each call runs one verification step and updates a confirmation code $\mathcal{C} = (\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{pub}}, \mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{priv}})$. The public part holds the step counter $j$, a failure flag that stays set once set, and the scheme's declared confidence $\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(j)$. The private part holds the verifier's own state, such as its coins, and never leaves it. An application-supplied policy $\mathtt{ProcessC}$ reads $\mathcal{C}^{\mathrm{pub}}$ and answers accept, reject or continue. $\mathtt{Verify}$ is the mechanism; $\mathtt{ProcessC}$ is the policy.

    For FRI, a step is one whole query (Con. 4). In the provable regime, one query passes a word that is $\delta$-far from the code with probability at most $\alpha_\circ$, which is $7/12$ at the puzzle's parameters once $\delta \ge 5/12$ (Thm. 2). Against a prover with a single attempt, $t$ passing queries would leave a cheating chance of at most $\alpha_\circ^{t}$. But a Fiat–Shamir prover does not get one attempt. Its query positions come from hashing its own proof, so it can hash again. Toggle between the two pictures below.

      A toy model, not the paper's experiment. Each attempt checks queries in order and each passes independently with probability $7/12$. That is the per-query rate of the grinding attack in Appendix B of the paper at $\delta = 5/12$, where it meets the provable threshold. Like that attack, the toy assumes a far word that survives the commit phase. Attempts are drawn by this page's seeded generator.

      The real prover's budget is enormous. $Q = 2^{80}$ hashes and a 20-bit proof-of-work leave about $Q\,2^{-w} = 2^{60}$ attempts with a valid nonce. Each derives its own query set (Thm. 2, (H2)). Their union bound $Q\,2^{-w}\alpha_\circ^{t}$ stays at or above 1 until $t_0 \approx (\log_2 Q - w)/(-\log_2 \alpha_\circ)$, which is 77 at these parameters (§5.2). Appendix B of the paper shows the effect is a real attack, not slack in the union bound. That attack uses the $1-\delta$ branch of $\alpha_\circ$ and meets the provable threshold at $\delta = 5/12$. It does not show that 77 itself is tight.

      So how should soundness be stated, when the verifier can stop anywhere and the prover can retry? It has to be a separate promise for every prefix.

      3 · The definition that falls out

      Soundness as a function of how far you got

      Definition 7 (Progressive soundness)

      A non-interactive argument $\Pi$ in the random-oracle model, whose verifier runs steps $V_0, \ldots, V_T$, has $(T, \alpha_{\mathrm{prog}})$-progressive soundness if for every $Q$, every $t \le T$, and every adversary making at most $Q$ random-oracle queries and outputting a statement $x$, a proof $\pi$ and an interruption point $t^{*} \le T$,

      $$\Pr\bigl[\,x \text{ is false} \;\wedge\; V_{\le t}(x, \pi) \text{ accepts} \;\wedge\; t^{*} = t\,\bigr] \;\le\; 1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t, Q).$$
      $$\begin{aligned}&\Pr\bigl[\,x \text{ is false} \;\wedge\; V_{\le t}(x, \pi) \text{ accepts}\\ &\qquad \wedge\; t^{*} = t\,\bigr] \;\le\; 1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t, Q).\end{aligned}$$

      Here $V_{\le t}$ runs only $V_0, \ldots, V_t$, and $\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}$ is non-decreasing in $t$ and efficiently computable. There is no asymptotic slack term: the residual oracle terms live inside $\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}$.

      The bound is on the joint event with $t^{*} = t$, one $t$ at a time. That is what lets a per-prefix guarantee stand against a prover that stops at its longest passing prefix. Theorem 2 gives FRI's curve. Under hypotheses (H1) on the pre-query rounds, (H2) on the query positions (independent, uniform, checked in derivation order) and (H3) on the proximity regime ($m \ge 3$),

      $$\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t, Q) = 1 - \min\Bigl\{1,\; Q \cdot 2^{-w} \cdot \alpha_\circ^{\,t} + \varepsilon_c(Q)\Bigr\},$$
      $$\alpha_\circ = \max\Bigl\{1-\delta,\; \sqrt{\rho}\,\bigl(1 + \tfrac{1}{2m}\bigr)\Bigr\}.$$

      Here $\varepsilon_c(Q) = Q \cdot \max_i \varepsilon_i + 3(Q^2+1)/2^{\kappa}$ collects the $Q$-inflated commit-phase error and the compilation term, with $\kappa$ the random-oracle output length, and $Q$ counts every random-oracle call, those the verifier makes on the presented proof included.

        Theorem 2 has one hypothesis that a careless implementation can break: the queries must be checked in the order they were derived. Why does the order matter, if the set of positions is the same?

        4 · Order matters

        The same positions, checked sorted, lose the prefix bound

        A deployed verifier, Winterfell, sorts and deduplicates the query positions and authenticates all their openings at once (§5.4). A batched multi-opening checks the $t$ smallest sampled positions first. Proposition 3 shows the $\alpha_\circ^{t}$ bound fails for them.             From Prop. 3: the derivation-order prefix passes with probability $(1-\delta)^{t}$, and the sorted prefix passes whenever at most $s - t$ of the $s$ samples land in the corrupted fraction. The expression $\Pr[\mathrm{Bin}(s, \delta) \le s - t]$ is this page's exact computation of that event for independent uniform positions (H2). At $t = s$ both check everything and agree. Prop. 3 names the deterministic core of the sorted case, sorted_prefix_avoids, and adds that Ligero's columns behave the same way. At $s = 140$, $\delta = 5/12$ the sorted prefix passes with probability about $0.96$ at $t = 72$ and about $0.51$ at $t = 82 \approx (1-\delta)s$ computed, so “near 1” holds for $t$ somewhat below $(1-\delta)s$.

            With the definition and its two traps in hand, what does the model buy across verifiers?

            The design space (§5.2, §6). Among the instantiations of Table 1, every route to validity confidence that rises exponentially with cheap steps pays with amortization across claims (beacon Freivalds), prover re-encoding of the authenticated object (FRI and Ligero), or verifier secrecy backed by setup work done before the claim arrives, which gives up public verifiability (the Freivalds compiler). Hash-based flexible signatures are public but linear. Whether public, per-claim, prover-unassisted exponential confidence is achievable for native matrix–vector verification is open.

            From a per-step bound to a policy

            Take a sound policy whose earliest accepting step is $t_P$; the per-step bound at $t_P$ carries over to sessions (Thm. 1). For a progressively unforgeable scheme with fresh coins, $N$ sessions cost a factor $N$: some session accepts a forgery with probability at most $N\,(1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t_P) + \nu(\lambda))$. For a progressively sound Fiat–Shamir argument the bound is $1 - \alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}(t_P, Q)$ whatever $N$, because $Q$ already counts every oracle call, the verifier's included. The fresh-coin factor is nearly attained: a distance-one Lamport–Diffie forgery shown to $N = 16$ sessions at $t_P = n - 1$, $n = 256$, is accepted with probability $0.061$, against the bound $0.0625$ (§4.2; calculator in the appendix).

            The reject side: naysayer proofs

            Naysayer proofs (Seres et al.) convince anyone that a claim is invalid, with less work than verifying it. They work on the reject side; progressive verification works on the accept side. A verifier with a public check decomposition $\phi_0, \ldots, \phi_K$ (Def. 4: deterministic checks of the claim, all of which hold exactly when it is valid) compiles into a naysayer that names one failing check in $\lceil \log_2(K+1) \rceil$ bits, complete and perfectly sound (Prop. 1). This still works when private coins choose the order of the checks. What no naysayer can do for a secret-coin verifier is certify the run. Take a verifier that is accept-perfect and reject-complete, with step outcomes that depend on its private coins, and a public, stateless, deterministic $\mathtt{VerifyNay}$ whose inputs are the claim and the refutation. On invalid and honestly generated valid inputs, its outcome is then determined by the base scheme's validity predicate alone, up to negligible error, and it cannot certify a per-run event, such as “step $j$ of this run failed”, whose probability $p$ over the verifier's coins has $p(1-p)$ non-negligible (Lemma 1).

            So the model makes these verifiers comparable. Where does it stop?

            6 · Where this goes next

            Open questions, and what the model leaves out

            Open (§6)

            • Whether public verifiability with exponentially rising confidence and cheap verification steps is achievable per claim, without prover assistance, for native matrix–vector verification.
            • Whether bounds beyond the Johnson radius (Dao et al.) shorten the dead zone at deployed parameters. The paper has not checked their commit-phase term at these parameters.
            • What a principled composition of heuristic, contextual confidence (the systems-side meaning of progressive authentication, where a device fuses sensor signals) and cryptographic confidence into one policy-facing quantity should look like. The paper calls this the central question its bridge opens.

            What the model leaves out, and where it does not apply

            • For the Fiat–Shamir arguments, $\alpha_{\mathrm{prog}}$ is a soundness level relative to the declared proximity regime and adversary budget, not a per-instance posterior probability (§5.2).
            • The confidence reported at the stopping step is calibrated when the stopping point is fixed before the session's challenges exist and does not depend on step outcomes, as for a deadline when step times do not depend on them. It need not be otherwise: a Fiat–Shamir prover can stop at its longest passing prefix. The bound attaches to the policy's acceptance step $t_P$ (§4.2).
            • Physical side channels, such as timing that depends on a secret verifier state, are outside the model (the paper's App. A).
            • Progressive verification is a property of natively decomposable verification. A single Pedersen vector-commitment opening is one multi-scalar multiplication, with no native decomposition into progressively checkable steps (§5.3).

            Appendix

            Calculators and notes

            Calculator 1. Reusing a Freivalds state (Prop. 2)

            A verifier that precomputes a secret state of $k$ rows and reuses it across $N$ sessions leaks information through early outcomes. Session $i$ reaches depth $t$ if its residual is nonzero yet passes rows $1, \ldots, t$. For $N \le q$, Prop. 2 bounds the chance that some session reaches depth $t$ by $\binom{N+t-1}{t}(q-N+1)^{-t} + q^{k-T}$ when the first failing row leaks (item 1), and by $\bigl(N/(q-N+1)\bigr)^{t} + q^{k-T}$ when every row's outcome leaks (item 2). Item 3 gives an attack that, seeing one accept bit per session, reaches depth $t$ (for $t \lt T$) within an expected number of sessions below $q\,t(t+1)/2 + t\,q^{2}/(q-1)^{2}$; it is shown only for free residuals, and whether a UOV residual can follow it is not checked (§5.1). The calculator omits $q^{k-T}$, which is negligible when $T \gg k$; the paper's example also leaves $T$ unstated.

            Calculator 2. A policy over $N$ fresh sessions (Thm. 1)

            A distance-one Lamport–Diffie forgery survives $t_P = n-1$ checks with probability $1/n$. Shown to $N$ fresh sessions, it is accepted by some session with probability $1-(1-1/n)^{N}$; Theorem 1 bounds this by $N/n$.

            Note 3. Ligero, as a sketch

            The same prefix accounting fits Fiat–Shamir Ligero, with one column, opened and run through all three tests, in the role of one FRI query. The per-column pass rate is $\alpha_\circ^{\mathrm{Lig}} = \max\{1 - e/n,\, (k+\ell)/n,\, 2k/n\} = 2/3$ at the optimal parameters, about 0.585 bits per column, and the three tests' union costs $\log_2 3$ bits. Published Ligero specifies no proof-of-work, so $w = 0$ and the dead zone is large: $t_0 \approx 139$ columns at $Q = 2^{80}$, against the about 219 columns the 128-bit parameter formula gives, which cap the confidence at about 46.5 bits. The paper marks this a sketch, not a theorem on the footing of Theorem 2: it takes the round-by-round Fiat–Shamir analysis of the extended Ligero paper as given.

            Note 4. What is machine-checked

            Proposition 2, items 1 and 2 including their Step 2, is mechanized in Lean for a deterministic adversary given as a decision tree over at most $N$ sequential sessions, the model to which Step 1 reduces. Step 1 itself is not mechanized. For item 3, only the arithmetic of the attack and one dimension bound are mechanized; the attack's correctness, its probabilities and its expectation are not (the paper's App. A, “Mechanization”).
