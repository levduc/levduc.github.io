ESORICS 2019

Flexible Signatures: Making Authentication Suitable for Real-Time Environments

When a signature check is cut off halfway, what has the verifier learned? For RSA or ECDSA, nothing. We define signatures whose verification can be stopped at any step and still reports how valid the signature is. We build one from Lamport–Diffie one-time signatures and a Merkle tree, and we prove how much security each step buys.

Duc V. Le1, Mahimna Kelkar2, Aniket Kate1

1Purdue University  ·  2Cornell University (this research was completed at Purdue University)

1 · The puzzle

Half a check. How much does it catch?

A Lamport–Diffie signature on a 4-bit digest reveals one secret per bit. Anyone can check a revealed secret by hashing it and comparing with the public key. The signer signed a message $m$ with digest $G(m)=\texttt{1011}$. A forger wants to pass off a different message $m^*$ with digest $\texttt{1001}$. It can reuse the three secrets it has already seen. At position 3 it would need a secret that was never revealed, so it puts garbage there. The verifier checks positions in a random order, and after two checks it is interrupted. How likely is it that the forgery is accepted?

Your guess: the forgery passes with probability …

Every pair of positions the verifier might check

 

The forger survives unless a check lands on position 3. Three of the six pairs avoid it, so the odds are $\tfrac12$, and the verifier that stopped early returns $\alpha=\tfrac24$. The forger's only lever is the distance between the two digests. At real sizes ($n=256$), digests that differ in just one bit are astronomically expensive to find. That trade-off is the whole paper.

The digest $\texttt{1011}$ and the four verifier outputs $\alpha=\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1$ come from the 4-bit example in the ESORICS talk. The forger's digest $\texttt{1001}$ is our choice for this puzzle. The odds are Equation (3) of the paper with $n=4$, $d=1$, $k_F=2$.

2 · Why did that happen? · Figure 2

The verifier knows where it looked. The forger knows where it lied.

A forger who saw one signature knows the secret for every position where its digest agrees with the signed one. It fails at each position where the digests differ, unless it breaks the hash function $F$. Call the number of such positions $d$, the Hamming distance between $G(m)$ and $G(m^*)$. The verifier of Figure 2 picks positions uniformly at random without repeats. It returns $\bot$ the moment a check fails. If it is interrupted after $k_F$ checks, it returns $\alpha=k_F/n$. Switch between the two points of view. Neither party sees the whole picture, and that is why partial checking works.

Point of view
    Table view — this run, position by position

    Toy parameters: $n=16$, random digests. The algorithm is Figure 2 (flexible verification) of the paper; the probability is Equation (3). In Theorem 1 the forger can also win by finding a preimage of $F$, which adds the $4n\cdot\epsilon_{ow}$ term. We leave that term out here.

    So a forger wants $d$ small: at $d=0$ it would pass every check. But two digests that are close are hard to find. Finding a pair within distance $\ell$ takes at least $t_{\ell\text{-}ncr}=2^{n/2}\big/\sqrt{\sum_{i=0}^{\ell}\binom{n}{i}}$ hash evaluations (Lamberger et al., §2). Section 3 turns that trade-off into a definition and a number.

    3 · The definition that falls out · Definitions 1–2, Theorem 1

    Validity instead of yes/no, and security for every validity

    We keep the three algorithms of a standard signature scheme and change what verification returns. To model an interrupt nobody plans for, we give the verifier an interruption oracle. The oracle says after how many computations $r$ the check will be cut off. The verifier must not use $r$ to bias the order of its checks.

    Definition 1 (flexible signature scheme, abridged)

    $\Sigma=(\mathsf{Gen},\mathsf{Sign},\mathsf{Ver})$, where $\mathsf{Gen}$ and $\mathsf{Sign}$ are as usual. $\mathsf{Ver}(pk,m,\sigma,\llbracket r\rrbracket)$ is probabilistic. It takes an optional interruption position $r\in\{0,\dots,\mathsf{max}\}$, and if $r$ is not given it asks $\mathsf{iOracle}_\Sigma(1^\lambda)$. It outputs $\alpha\in[0,1]\cup\{\bot\}$, where $\alpha=0$ means no operation was performed. Correctness: an honest signature is never rejected, whatever $r$ is: $\Pr[\mathsf{Ver}(pk,m,\mathsf{Sign}(sk,m),r)=\bot]=0$.

    An extracting function $\mathsf{iExtract}_\Sigma(\alpha)$ recovers $r$ from $\alpha$. For the Lamport–Diffie construction it is $\lfloor\alpha\cdot n\rfloor$.

    Definition 2 (security at validity $\alpha$), via the experiment $\mathsf{FlexExp}_{\mathcal A,\Sigma}(1^\lambda,\alpha)$
    1. The challenger runs $\mathsf{Gen}$, computes $r=\mathsf{iExtract}_\Sigma(\alpha)$ and sends $(pk,r)$ to $\mathcal A$.
    2. $\mathcal A$ asks for signatures on messages of its choice.
    3. $\mathcal A$ outputs $(m^*,\sigma^*)$ with $m^*$ never queried.
    4. $\mathcal A$ wins if $\alpha^*=\mathsf{Ver}(pk,m^*,\sigma^*,r)\neq\bot$ and $\alpha^*\geq\alpha$.

    $\Sigma$ is $(t,\epsilon,q)$-EUF-CMA if every $\mathcal A$ running in time $t$ with $q$ queries wins with probability at most $\epsilon$. Here $t$ and $\epsilon$ are functions of $\alpha$ and $\lambda$.

    From odds to bits: the security level at $n=256$

    Theorem 1 bounds the forger's time $t$ and success probability $\epsilon$ for every distance $\ell\le n-k_F$ it might aim for. §6.1 turns the bound into a number. It assumes a generic birthday-style near-collision search, and it takes $d$ to be the expected distance of a pair found within $\ell$ (Equation (1)). The security level is the forger's best choice:

    $$\begin{gathered}\mathrm{SL}(k_F)=\min_{0\le\ell\le n-k_F}\log_2\frac{t}{\epsilon}\\ t=\max\Big\{1,\;\frac{2^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i\le\ell}\binom{n}{i}}}-4n\Big\}\\ \epsilon=\min\Big\{1,\;2\prod_{i=0}^{k_F-1}\Big(1-\frac{d}{n-i}\Big)\Big\}\end{gathered}$$

        4 · What it buys, part 1 · Figures 3, 4, 6 and Theorem 2

        Many messages: a flexible Merkle tree

        A Merkle signature carries a one-time signature, its one-time public key $\mathsf{PK}_s$, and the authentication path $\mathsf{Auth}_s$ that links $\mathsf{PK}_s$ to the root. We also make the signer send the complement nodes $\mathsf{Auth}^c_s$, which lie on the direct path itself. With both, the verifier can check any level of the tree on its own, in any order. It can even check levels in parallel. There are two kinds of work. Checking a Lamport position with $F$ gives exponential confidence $1-1/2^{k_F/2}$. Checking a tree level with $H$ gives only linear confidence $k_H/(h+1)$, because a forger can bring its own one-time key and break just one level. Figure 4 balances the two by checking with $F$ whenever $1-1/2^{k_F/2}\le k_H/(h+1)$ and with $H$ otherwise.

          The price of linear authenticity, at $h=20$

          Theorem 2 carries the linear term into the bound. With $k_H\lt h+1$, $\epsilon_{fms}$ includes $1-\frac{k_H}{h+1}$ and $t_{fms}=\mathcal O(1)$. The forger also gets $2^h$ one-time instances to aim at. Figure 6 shows the result for $n=256$, $h=20$, so $n+h+1=277$ computations in total. The level stays near 1 bit for the first ~76 computations, then climbs to 80 bits at about $\tfrac23$ of the way.

            printed: $t_{fms}=\max\{1,\,t_{\ell\text{-}ncr}-2^{h+\log_2(h+1)n}-2^{h\cdot\log_2 2n}-2^{\log_2(n-h-1)}\}$
            our reading: $t_{fms}=\max\{1,\,t_{\ell\text{-}ncr}-2^{h+\log_2(h+1)n}-2^{h+\log_2 2n}-(n+h+1)\}$

            5 · What it buys, part 2 · §6.3, Table 1

            On a Raspberry Pi: security you hold at every moment

            We implemented both constructions in C with OpenSSL's SHA-256 and ran them on a Raspberry Pi 3 Model B with 1GB of RAM, verifying messages of size 256. The standard schemes were measured with OpenSSL (RSA with public exponent $2^{16}+1$). A standard verifier has one point on this chart: nothing until it finishes. A flexible verifier has a curve, and wherever it is stopped it knows its security level.

            Verification time vs. security level (Table 1)

              Table 1, exactly as printed: pairs are (time, security level in bits) at 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the verification computations. Security levels of the standard schemes are from NIST and Buchmann et al., as cited in the paper. The paper's own explanation of the Merkle row is that $H(\mathsf{PK}_{fots})$ and $G(m)$ are costly. SHA-2's Merkle–Damgård structure makes them call the compression function many times, so a shorter partial check saves less time than it does for Lamport.

              These are two constructions of a new notion. The paper ends by asking where else the idea applies.

              6 · Where this goes next · §5.3, §7

              What the model leaves open

              • Other hash-based schemes. We chose Lamport–Diffie because its verification always takes the same number of hashes, which gives precise proofs, and because it verifies fast. The same technique applies to XMSS, SPHINCS, HORS, BIBA, HORST and Winternitz, but the security analysis of each is still to be done.
              • Number-theoretic and lattice signatures. Fiat–Shamir signatures split into separately verifiable sets, but a modular exponentiation costs much more than a hash. GPV lattice signatures verify with a matrix–vector product, so checking random rows looks applicable. We name a flexible GPV signature as future work.
              • The first few computations of a Merkle check. Authenticity grows only linearly, and $2^h$ one-time instances make cheap near-collisions useful to the forger. So the first steps buy almost no security (Fig. 6).
              • The security levels are estimates. They assume a generic near-collision attack, and that the forger's distance equals its expected value given $\ell$ (§6.1). The theorems themselves hold for any $d\le\ell$.
              • Beyond signatures. Similar flexible guarantees for confidentiality with symmetric or asymmetric encryption, and for integrity with MACs (§7).
